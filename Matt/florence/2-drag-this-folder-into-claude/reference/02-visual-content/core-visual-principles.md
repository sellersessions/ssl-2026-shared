# Core Visual Principles

> **Scope:** The 6 foundational principles that apply to EVERY visual asset on an Amazon listing — main image, listing images, and A+ Content. These are non-negotiable. Everything in the other visual content files builds on these principles.
>
> **This is the single source of truth for core principles.** The main-image, listing-images, and a-plus-content files reference these principles but do not repeat them in full. If a principle needs to be updated, update it here.

---

## Principle 1: Show Me, Don't Tell Me

This is the single most important principle in Amazon visual content.

**Always lead with photography that visually demonstrates the benefit.** A shopper should understand the core benefit of your product from the image alone — before reading a single word of copy. If you have to explain the benefit with text, the image isn't doing its job.

The photograph IS the proof. Put the product in the situation where the benefit is undeniable.

### What This Looks Like

| Product Claim | Wrong Approach (Telling) | Right Approach (Showing) |
|---|---|---|
| Waterproof raincoat | Text overlay: "100% Waterproof" on flat lay | Photo of the coat in heavy rain, water beading off the surface |
| Powerful flashlight | Spec callout: "5000 lumens" | Photo of the beam lighting up an entire forest at night |
| Ultra-sharp knife | Bullet list of blade features | Photo of the knife slicing cleanly through a tomato, paper-thin |
| Lightweight backpack | "Only 1.2 lbs!" text badge | Photo of someone holding it effortlessly with one finger |
| Stain-resistant fabric | "Stain-proof technology" graphic | Photo of coffee spilling onto the fabric and beading up |

The pattern: **put the product in the situation where the benefit is undeniable.** The photograph IS the proof. The copy just names what the shopper already sees.

### The Test

For every image, ask: "Would the shopper understand the benefit if I removed all the text?" If the answer is no, the photography isn't carrying the message.

## Principle 2: Photography First, Copy Second

Photography carries the message. Copy supports it.

- Every image starts with a photograph that makes the benefit self-evident
- Copy is only there to reinforce what the eye already sees
- Short, bold statements — 3-6 words per callout, not sentences
- If a callout needs a full sentence to make sense, the photo isn't doing enough
- Think labels, not explanations: "Bone Dry. Every Time." not "Our proprietary waterproof membrane keeps you dry in all weather conditions"
- The best visual content is **70%+ photography with minimal, punchy copy** layered on top
- Infographics are for specs and comparisons that can't be photographed. Benefits belong in photographs.

---

## Principle 3: Clean, Focused, Scannable

Every image should communicate its message in under 2 seconds.

- No cluttered backgrounds — the product and benefit are the only focus
- Not too dark, not too busy, not overwhelming — bright, clean, and easy on the eyes
- One clear focal point per image — if the shopper's eye doesn't know where to land, the image fails
- Think of each image as a billboard: if you can't understand it at a glance, simplify it
- White space is a feature, not wasted space — it directs the eye
- One message per image or segment — don't try to say everything on one slide

---

## Principle 4: Right Demographic

The person in the image must match the target customer.

- Age, gender, ethnicity, setting — all should reflect who actually buys the product
- A skincare product for women in their 40s should show a woman in her 40s, not a 22-year-old model
- An outdoor product for weekend hikers should show regular people on a trail, not professional athletes on a summit
- Wrong demographic = the shopper doesn't see themselves in the image = they scroll past
- When in doubt, check the review photos and Rufus queries — real customers show you exactly who they are. _(SellerApp's Q&A endpoint is deprecated in v0.1.5; review imagery + Rufus shopper questions are the live signals.)_

### How to Identify the Right Demographic

- **Photo reviews:** Real customers posting photos of themselves with the product — this is your ground truth
- **Keyword long-tails:** "for women over 40," "for small apartments," "for beginners" — these reveal who the buyer is
- **Review language:** How they describe themselves, their situation, their needs

---

## Principle 5: Mobile-First

80%+ of Amazon shoppers browse on mobile. If it doesn't work on a phone, it doesn't work.

- All text must be legible at phone screen size — minimum 24pt equivalent
- High contrast between text and background — avoid light text on light product photos
- One message per image or segment — don't try to cram everything into one frame
- Test every image by viewing it at 400x400px on your phone
- Full-width images and layouts outperform side-by-side on mobile

### The Mobile Test

View every image and A+ segment at phone screen size before publishing. If you have to squint, zoom, or tilt to read anything — it fails.

---

## Principle 6: Visual Consistency

The entire listing should feel like one cohesive brand experience.

- Same fonts, colors, and graphic style across all listing images
- Consistent background treatment across the gallery (all white, all with brand color accents, etc.)
- A+ Content should extend the visual language of the listing images, not introduce a new one
- Brand colors should accent, not dominate — the product and photography are the heroes
- The image gallery and A+ Content should look like they were designed as a set, not assembled from different sources

### Consistency Checklist

- Same font family across all images
- Same color palette (2-3 brand accent colors max)
- Same callout style (icon + text, or text only — pick one and commit)
- Same background treatment
- Same photography style (lighting, tone, mood)

## The Anti-Template Principle

There is no universal template for what visual content goes where. Every product is different. Every customer is different.

Before deciding on any visual content — image stack, A+ layout, anything — you must first:

1. **Understand the customer** — Who are they? What do they care about? What are they comparing you to?
2. **Understand buyer intent** — Why are they searching? What problem are they solving? What outcome do they want?
3. **Identify their questions** — What do they need to know before buying? What are they worried about? What's holding them back?
4. **Know the product's benefits and features** — What actually matters? What differentiates it? What makes it worth the price?
5. **Then decide the visual content.** Not before.

The research drives the content. The content does not come from a formula.

## Common Mistakes (Across All Visual Content)

These mistakes violate the core principles above. They appear in main images, listing images, and A+ Content. Ranked by frequency and impact:

1. **Too Much Text** — Infographics crammed with paragraphs. A+ segments that are walls of text. Cut the text in half, then cut it in half again. *(Violates Principles 2, 3)*
2. **Telling Instead of Showing** — Writing "100% Waterproof" instead of photographing the product in the rain. If the benefit can be photographed, it must be photographed. *(Violates Principle 1)*
3. **Wrong Demographic** — Models or settings that don't match the target customer. *(Violates Principle 4)*
4. **Generic Stock Photography** — Obvious stock photos that don't match the product or brand. Customers recognize stock photos instantly. They erode trust. *(Violates Principles 1, 4)*
5. **Ignoring Mobile** — Text readable on desktop but illegible on a phone. Always test mobile first. *(Violates Principle 5)*
6. **Inconsistent Design** — Each image looks like a different designer made it. *(Violates Principle 6)*
7. **Feature Dumping** — Listing every spec instead of focusing on benefits. Lead with outcomes, not inputs. *(Violates Principles 1, 2)*
8. **Dark, Cluttered, Overwhelming** — Too dark, too busy, too many competing elements. *(Violates Principle 3)*
9. **Wasting Slot 7** — Last visual before the buy decision. It should close strong. *(Violates the Anti-Template Principle — slot 7 should be research-driven)*
10. **Copying a Template** — Following a generic formula without considering the specific product and customer. *(Violates the Anti-Template Principle)*
